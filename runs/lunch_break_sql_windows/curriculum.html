Assumptions I made
  • Self-join reports are typically things like previous-row comparisons, running totals, and top-N per group, which map to LAG/LEAD, window frames, and ROW_NUMBER/RANK.
  • Videos should use PostgreSQL or standard SQL syntax that works in PostgreSQL.
  • Two videos at most means the plan should favor one comprehensive practical video plus optionally one short complementary video, not many small ones.
  • No practice time is reserved, so the 30 minutes is all watching.
  1. OVER() and PARTITION BY: window vs GROUP BY
    The learner knows GROUP BY aggregates, so the key mental shift is keeping row-level detail while computing aggregates over partitions, which replaces self-joins to aggregated subqueries.
  2. ROW_NUMBER, RANK, DENSE_RANK for top-N per group
    Top-N per group and deduplication are common self-join/subquery report patterns that ranking functions replace directly.
  3. LAG and LEAD for previous/next row comparisons
    Comparing a row with its previous or next row is the classic self-join use case, and LAG/LEAD make it a single pass.
  4. Running totals and window frames (ORDER BY, ROWS/RANGE BETWEEN)
    Running totals and moving averages usually require painful self-joins with inequality conditions; frames replace them.
  5. Rewriting self-join reports as window function queries
    The goal is to convert existing reports, so seeing before/after examples of self-join to window function rewrites ties everything together.

Window functions are the moment SQL clicks for most analysts. Rankings, running totals, month-over-month growth and moving averages, all without a single self join. In the next 18 minutes, you'll learn every window function you'll actually use at work, live on a real store database. Chapter one, what a window function is, an aggregate that keeps every row.

Group by collapses rows. You get one row per group, and the detail is gone. A window function keeps every row and adds a calculated value next to each one. Every window function has the same shape. A function, the word over, and brackets that describe the window, how to split the rows and how to order them.

Partition by decides which rows belong together. Order by decides the sequence inside each group. Our store has 314 orders from January to June, from 150 customers.

Each order has a customer, a product, a quantity, a date, and a status. I've also saved revenue per month in a small table called monthly. We'll use it for growth and running totals. Six months. January brought in 34,516, and June 67,727.

Chapter two, aggregates with OVER, totals and averages on every row. Empty brackets after over means the window is the whole table. So every row shows the grand total, 293,147, and we still have all six months.

Because the total sits on every row, percent of total is one division away. June alone is 23.1% of all six months. No subquery needed. Partition by city gives each city its own window.

Now every customer sits next to their city's average. So you can instantly see who spends above or below their city's average. Your turn, show each customer's spend next to the overall average spend. Pause the video if you need more time.

Welcome to Tech Brothers IT. In this video, we answer the SQL interview questions about running totals, LAG and LEAD, the window functions behind every trend report. Every answer is a complete query with its real output, and the last one is the monthly growth report a manager will ask you to write on the spot.

Question one: how do you calculate a running total of revenue by date? SUM as a window function, with ORDER BY inside the OVER clause. Each row gets its own amount plus every row before it. No self join, and no subquery. The first order is one thousand two hundred and fifty. The second adds three hundred, so the running total is one thousand five hundred and fifty, and so on down the dates.

And here's a sanity check you should always do in a real project: the last running total, four thousand seven hundred and fifty, must equal the total revenue for the period. Remember what ORDER BY does inside OVER. It doesn't just sort. It sets a frame, from the first row up to the current row. That frame is the trap in the next question.

Question two: how do you calculate a running total per customer, and what's the difference between ROWS and RANGE? Per customer is one more clause: PARTITION BY customer. The running total restarts for every customer. Mei Chen goes six hundred and fifty, nine hundred and twenty five, one thousand one hundred and seventy five.

Then Omar starts again from three hundred. Now the trap. When you write ORDER BY without a frame, the default frame is RANGE, from the first row to the current row. And RANGE includes every row with the same ORDER BY value, its peers. Order 1003 was paid in two parts. Ordered by order ID, both payments are peers, so the default frame adds them together: two thousand two hundred twice.

The fix: make the ORDER BY unique, and write ROWS explicitly. Now it goes one thousand nine hundred and fifty, then two thousand two hundred. Why both? ROWS alone splits the tie, but in no guaranteed order. Question three: how do you show the month over month change in revenue?

First, a CTE with revenue per month. DATE_TRUNC turns every date into the first day of its month. That's Postgres. In SQL Server, use DATE TRUNC, or DATEFROMPARTS on older versions. Then LAG reads the previous row's revenue, and LEAD reads the next row's. The change is simply revenue minus LAG of revenue.

February made one thousand eight hundred and fifty. January one thousand five hundred and fifty, so the change is plus three hundred. March dropped by one thousand one hundred and seventy five. January has no previous month, so LAG returns NULL. The pitfall: LAG means the previous row, not the previous month. If a month had no sales, it wouldn't exist in the CTE, and LAG would compare with two months back.

In a real report, join to a calendar of months first.

Question four: how do you calculate a three month moving average? AVG as a window function, with an explicit frame: ROWS BETWEEN TWO PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW. That's this month plus the two before it. March averages January, February and March: one thousand three hundred and fifty eight point three three.

April drops January and adds April: nine hundred and fifty. Now look at the first two rows. January averages one month, and February two. I added a COUNT over the same frame to make that visible. If the business wants a true three month average, return NULL until months used is three. Same warning as LAG: ROWS counts rows, not calendar months, so fill the missing months first.

In a real project, this is what you put on a dashboard to smooth a noisy trend line.

Question five: how do you calculate each category's percent of total revenue, without a subquery? Group revenue by category, as usual. Then SUM of SUM, OVER with empty brackets. The inner SUM is the normal group total. The outer SUM is a window over all the grouped rows, so every row gets the grand total: four thousand seven hundred and fifty.

Divide one by the other. Electronics is sixty three point one six percent. Furniture thirty two point six three, and Accessories four point two one. Why does SUM of SUM work? Because window functions run after GROUP BY. That's the logical query order from the first video of this series, and interviewers love to hear you connect the two.

Two details: multiply by one hundred point zero, so integer columns don't do integer division. And in a real report, check that the percentages add up to one hundred.

Question six: what do FIRST_VALUE and LAST_VALUE do, and why does LAST_VALUE seem not to work? For each employee in Sales and Engineering, we want the top paid and the lowest paid person in the department. FIRST_VALUE, ordered by salary descending, gives the top: Daniel in Sales, Omar in Engineering.

Correct on every row. LAST_VALUE with the same window should give the lowest paid. But look at the last default column: every row just returns its own name. That's the default frame again. With ORDER BY, the frame ends at the current row, or its last tie, so here the last value in the frame is the current row itself.

The fix: extend the frame to UNBOUNDED FOLLOWING, the whole partition. Now every row shows Fatima in Sales and Mei in Engineering. Even simpler, use FIRST_VALUE with the order reversed ascending.

And the scenario. Your manager asks: for every month, show the revenue, the previous month's revenue, the change and the percent growth, in one query. Build it in layers, because it's easier to read and to debug. CTE one: revenue per month. CTE two: add the previous month with LAG. Then the final select does the math on plain columns, so LAG is written only once.

Percent growth is the change divided by the previous revenue, times one hundred point zero. NULLIF of previous revenue and zero turns a zero into NULL, so a month after a zero month returns NULL, instead of a divide by zero error. February grew nineteen point three five percent. March fell sixty three point five one percent, and June fell eighty three point three three.

And two things to tell the manager. January is NULL on purpose: there's no previous month, so growth is unknown, not zero. And if a month ever has no sales, join to a calendar first, so LAG compares the right months.

To recap: SUM over ORDER BY gives a running total, and PARTITION BY restarts it per group. The default frame is RANGE: ties add up together. Write ROWS. LAG / LEAD read the previous / next row, not month. Frames drive moving averages and fix LAST_VALUE. SUM(SUM(x)) OVER() = percent of total; NULLIF for growth.
